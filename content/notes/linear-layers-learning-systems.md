## Introduction

A linear layer is one of the simplest objects in machine learning:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
z=Wx+b.
$$
</div>

It multiplies an input by a matrix and adds a bias. By itself, this operation cannot represent an arbitrary nonlinear relationship. Yet it is the basic trainable transformation inside classifiers, regressors, multilayer perceptrons, convolutional networks, recurrent networks, attention blocks, and many modern generative models.

The importance of the linear layer is therefore not that it is powerful in isolation. It is that it exposes, in the cleanest possible setting, almost every ingredient of a learning system:

- data must be represented numerically;
- predictions must be connected to targets;
- a loss must convert disagreement into an optimization objective;
- gradients must be computed and used to update parameters;
- performance must be measured on data that did not determine those parameters;
- model capacity must be controlled so that fitting becomes generalization;
- nonlinearities and architectural structure must be added when one affine map is not expressive enough.

This chapter follows that chain from beginning to end. The goal is not merely to know the formula for a linear layer. It is to understand what the formula assumes, how it is trained, why the optimization step can work, where it fails, and how the same ideas scale into a complete neural network.

## What Machine Learning Is Trying to Do

Let the observed dataset be

<div class="display-equation">
$$
S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n,
$$
</div>

where $x_i\in\mathcal{X}$ is an input and $y_i\in\mathcal{Y}$ is a target. A model is a parameterized function $f_\theta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}'$. Learning selects a parameter $\theta$ so that predictions are useful not only on the observed pairs, but also on future observations generated by the relevant environment.

This last clause is the difficult one. Memorizing $y_i$ for every observed $x_i$ is not the same as learning a rule that generalizes. The quantity we would ideally minimize is the population risk. Because the data-generating distribution $P$ is unknown, training replaces that expectation with an average over the observed sample:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
R(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\left[\ell(f_\theta(X),Y)\right],
\qquad
\widehat{R}_S(\theta)=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(f_\theta(x_i),y_i).
$$
</div>

The difference between these two quantities is the generalization problem. Optimization asks how well we minimize $\widehat{R}_S$. Statistical learning asks whether doing so also makes $R$ small. They are related, but they are not the same question.

### Supervised, semi-supervised, unsupervised, and self-supervised learning

The form of the available information determines the learning setting.

- **Supervised learning** observes input-target pairs $(x_i,y_i)$ and learns a predictive relationship.
- **Semi-supervised learning** combines a smaller labeled set with a larger unlabeled set. The unlabeled examples are useful only through assumptions about geometry, smoothness, clusters, consistency, or representation.
- **Unsupervised learning** observes inputs without externally supplied targets and seeks structure such as clusters, factors, densities, low-dimensional representations, or generative mechanisms.
- **Self-supervised learning** constructs a prediction task from the data itself. Masked-token prediction, next-token prediction, contrastive views, and reconstruction are examples. The generated target is not arbitrary; it is designed so that solving the proxy task requires a reusable representation.

These categories describe the information supplied to the learner, not mutually exclusive families of algorithms. A representation learned self-supervised can later be adapted with supervised data, and a probabilistic model may participate in all four settings.

### The three sources of error

It is useful to separate three reasons a trained model may perform poorly.

1. **Approximation error:** the model family cannot represent a sufficiently good predictor.
2. **Estimation error:** finite data do not identify the best member of that family accurately.
3. **Optimization error:** the training algorithm fails to find even the best parameters supported by the observed objective.

Adding parameters may reduce approximation error and increase estimation difficulty. Better optimization may reduce training loss without improving generalization. More data may reduce estimation error while leaving a misspecified model family unchanged. Keeping these mechanisms separate prevents vague diagnoses such as "the model did not learn."

## Binary Classification as Geometry

With the learning problem now defined, the simplest useful place to see all of its parts interact is binary classification. It turns abstract ideas about risk, representation, and optimization into a concrete geometric question: can two kinds of observations be separated in feature space?

Consider images of two handwritten digits. A $28\times28$ grayscale image can be flattened into a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^{784}$. The coordinates are pixel intensities. Flattening discards explicit two-dimensional neighborhood structure, but it gives a simple space in which to introduce linear classification.

An affine score has the form

<div class="display-equation">
$$
z(x)=w^\top x+b,
\qquad
w\in\mathbb{R}^{784},
\quad b\in\mathbb{R}.
$$
</div>

The decision boundary is the hyperplane

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\{x:w^\top x+b=0\}.
$$
</div>

The sign of the score determines the predicted side. The vector $w$ is normal to the hyperplane, while $b$ shifts it away from the origin. If $w\neq0$, the signed Euclidean distance from $x$ to the boundary is

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\frac{w^\top x+b}{\|w\|_2}.
$$
</div>

This geometric quantity is more informative than the sign alone. It gives a margin: points far from the boundary are separated more decisively than points near it.

### Linear separability and what it does not mean

A labeled dataset with $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ is linearly separable if some $w$ and $b$ make $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)$ positive for every training example.

If one separator exists, infinitely many scaled copies exist. A stronger formulation asks for a positive normalized margin. Large-margin methods use this distinction to prefer separators that remain stable under perturbation.

Linear separability is a property of the representation as well as the labels. Raw pixels may not be linearly separable even when a suitable learned representation is. The purpose of earlier layers in a neural network is often to transform data into a space where a final linear boundary becomes effective.

Nor does separability imply generalization. In a sufficiently high-dimensional space, random or idiosyncratic training labels may be separable. A boundary can classify every training point correctly and still encode no useful rule about future data.

## From Scores to Probabilities

A hard threshold produces a class but is unsuitable as a training interface. Its derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the threshold, so infinitesimal parameter changes provide no useful gradient signal.

For binary classification, the sigmoid function maps a score to the open interval $(0,1)$. Its derivative is largest near the decision boundary and approaches zero at extreme scores:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
p_\theta(x)
=
\sigma(z)
=
\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
\qquad
z=w^\top x+b,
\qquad
\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)).
$$
</div>

Interpreting $p_\theta(x)$ as $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Y=1\mid X=x)$ gives logistic regression. Probability itself is not assumed to be linear. Instead, the conditional log-odds $\log(p/(1-p))$ equal the affine score $w^\top x+b$.

### Why the loss matters

The zero-one loss $\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{y}\neq y\}$ matches the final classification error but is discontinuous and provides no graded information about how to improve the score. Training therefore uses a surrogate loss whose geometry is easier to optimize and whose minimization is connected to good classification.

For a Bernoulli target $y\in\{0,1\}$, the negative log-likelihood is binary cross-entropy:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(p,y)
=
-y\log p-(1-y)\log(1-p).
$$
</div>

Combining this loss with the sigmoid and writing it directly in terms of the raw logit $z$ gives

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\ell(z,y)=\log(1+e^z)-yz,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}
=
\sigma(z)-y.
$$
</div>

This identity is one reason the sigmoid and Bernoulli log-likelihood belong together. If mean squared error is placed after a sigmoid, the derivative contains an additional factor $\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, which can nearly vanish for a confidently wrong prediction. Binary cross-entropy does not remove every saturation problem in a deep network, but it supplies a better-conditioned output gradient for classification.

**A complete one-example gradient.**

For one observation, let $z=w^\top x+b$, $p=\sigma(z)$, and use binary cross-entropy. The chain rule gives $\nabla_w\ell=(p-y)x$ and $\partial\ell/\partial b=p-y$.

Take $x=(2,3)^\top$, $y=1$, $w=(1,2)^\top$, and $b=3$. Then $z=11$ and $p\approx0.999983$. The prediction, gradient, and update can be read as one continuous calculation:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\begin{aligned}
p-y&\approx-1.67\times10^{-5},\\
\nabla_w\ell&\approx
\begin{pmatrix}
-3.34\times10^{-5}\\
-5.01\times10^{-5}
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}\approx-1.67\times10^{-5},\\
w^+&=w-\eta\nabla_w\ell,
\qquad
b^+=b-\eta\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

With $\eta=0.1$, the update is small because this example is already classified with extreme confidence. A useful derivation should make the causal chain visible: parameters determine a score, the score determines a probability, the probability determines a loss, and the loss sends an error signal back through every dependency.

## Optimization: Why Gradient Descent Descends

Let $F(\theta)$ be the empirical objective. Gradient descent uses

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\theta_{k+1}
=
\theta_k-\eta\nabla F(\theta_k).
$$
</div>

Under the Euclidean norm, $-\nabla F(\theta)$ is the direction of steepest local decrease among unit directions. This is a local statement. It does not say that a fixed step of arbitrary length will decrease the objective, nor that a nonconvex objective has no undesirable stationary points.

### From Newton's method to first-order methods

Newton's method builds a local quadratic model and chooses the step by solving its first-order condition:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
F(\theta+s)
\approx
F(\theta)+\nabla F(\theta)^\top s
+\frac{1}{2}s^\top H(\theta)s,
\qquad
H(\theta)s=-\nabla F(\theta).
$$
</div>

This should be understood as a linear solve, not as an instruction to form $H^{-1}$ explicitly. For large neural networks, storing the Hessian is already prohibitive, and a dense factorization would be far too expensive. First-order methods replace curvature with a scalar step size or a cheaper preconditioner.

### The descent lemma

Assume that $F$ has an $L$-Lipschitz gradient:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\|\nabla F(u)-\nabla F(v)\|_2
\leq
L\|u-v\|_2.
$$
</div>

Define $g(t)=F(\theta+ts)$ and express the change in $F$ as the integral of $g'$. Add and subtract $\nabla F(\theta)$ inside that integral. Cauchy-Schwarz and gradient Lipschitzness then give

<div class="display-equation">
$$
F(\theta+s)
\leq
F(\theta)
+\langle\nabla F(\theta),s\rangle
+\frac{L}{2}\|s\|_2^2.
$$
</div>

This is the descent lemma. Substituting $s=-\eta\nabla F(\theta)$ gives the actual one-step guarantee:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
F(\theta-\eta\nabla F(\theta))
\leq
F(\theta)
-\eta\left(1-\frac{L\eta}{2}\right)
\|\nabla F(\theta)\|_2^2.
$$
</div>

Therefore every nonstationary step decreases the objective when $0\lt\eta\lt2/L$.

### What the theorem does and does not guarantee

For an $L$-smooth objective bounded below, the inequality shows that the objective values decrease and that the accumulated squared gradient norms are finite. Under standard fixed-step assumptions, this implies that gradients approach zero. It does **not** by itself prove convergence to a local minimum. A stationary point may be a local minimum, local maximum, saddle point, or a point on a flat manifold.

Additional structure gives stronger conclusions.

- If $F$ is convex, every local minimum is global, and gradient descent with a suitable step has a sublinear objective-value rate.
- If $F$ is strongly convex and smooth, the minimizer is unique and gradient descent converges linearly for an appropriate step size.
- If $F$ is nonconvex, smoothness provides descent and stationarity guarantees, not global optimality.

This distinction is important because deep-network objectives are generally nonconvex. Their practical trainability depends on architecture, initialization, stochasticity, normalization, overparameterization, and the geometry encountered along the optimization path, not on a blanket theorem that gradient descent always finds a local minimum.

### Stochastic gradients and minibatches

The full empirical gradient averages all observations. A minibatch $B_k$ uses a smaller sample:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\nabla\widehat{R}_S(\theta)=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\nabla\ell_i(\theta),
\qquad
g_k=
\frac{1}{|B_k|}\sum_{i\in B_k}\nabla\ell_i(\theta_k).
$$
</div>

With appropriate sampling, $g_k$ is an unbiased or nearly unbiased estimate of the full gradient. Its variance decreases with batch size, but computation and memory increase. Gradient noise is not merely a trick for escaping local minima. It changes the optimization trajectory, interacts with the learning rate, and can bias training toward regions with different local geometry.

## Backpropagation as Organized Chain Rule

A model is a composition of elementary maps. Backpropagation computes derivatives of a scalar loss with respect to many parameters by applying the chain rule in reverse topological order.

For the path $x\mapsto z=w^\top x+b\mapsto p=\sigma(z)\mapsto\ell(p,y)$, the backward pass begins with $\partial\ell/\partial\ell=1$ and repeatedly applies local derivatives. In tensor language, reverse mode propagates vector-Jacobian products. It avoids constructing a full Jacobian for every intermediate object.

If a node feeds several downstream paths, its total gradient is the sum of the contributions from those paths. In $L=xy+xz$, the variable $x$ reaches the loss along two branches, so

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}
=
\frac{\partial(xy)}{\partial x}
+
\frac{\partial(xz)}{\partial x}
=y+z.
$$
</div>

This accumulation rule is essential for residual connections, parameter sharing, recurrent networks, and any graph with branches.

Automatic differentiation is neither symbolic differentiation nor finite differencing. A framework records elementary operations during the forward pass and evaluates their derivative rules during the backward pass. The resulting graph is usually recreated on each eager execution, which permits ordinary control flow while preserving reverse-mode differentiation.

## Generalization and Honest Evaluation

Training performance answers a historical question: how well does the fitted model explain data that influenced its parameters? Deployment performance asks a predictive question: how well will the entire modeling procedure work on relevant future data? Honest evaluation keeps those questions separate.

### Training, validation, and test data

A conventional workflow uses three disjoint roles.

- The **training set** determines learned parameters through gradient updates.
- The **validation set** informs hyperparameters, architecture choices, stopping time, thresholds, and model selection.
- The **test set** estimates the performance of the finalized procedure and should be consulted only after major choices are fixed.

The exact percentages are not universal. They depend on dataset size, class balance, temporal structure, and the cost of uncertainty. What matters is the separation of roles.

Repeatedly selecting models according to test performance turns the test set into another validation set. The reported score then becomes optimistically biased. A fresh holdout, nested cross-validation, or an external evaluation set is required to recover an honest estimate.

Random splitting is also not always valid. Time series should usually respect temporal order. Measurements from the same patient, user, device, location, or document family may need group-level splitting. Near duplicates must not cross partitions. Preprocessing statistics, feature selection, and data augmentation policies must be fitted without looking at held-out targets.

Large-scale pretraining does not make validation unnecessary. It makes contamination control more difficult. A model can encounter benchmark items, paraphrases, or answer-bearing documents during pretraining. Evaluation must therefore track provenance, deduplication, temporal cutoffs, and benchmark exposure rather than assuming that dataset scale protects against overfitting.

### Underfitting, overfitting, and misdiagnosis

Underfitting is often described as high error on both training and validation data. Overfitting is often described as low training error and substantially worse validation error. These patterns are useful, but their causes are broader than model size alone.

High training loss may arise from insufficient capacity, poor features, excessive regularization, inadequate optimization, a learning rate that is too small or too large, label noise, or an objective that does not match the task. A large model can underfit if it is not optimized effectively.

A generalization gap may arise from excessive flexibility relative to data, repeated tuning on the validation set, distribution mismatch, leakage in training but not validation, spurious correlations, or simply high uncertainty in a small validation sample. A small model can overfit when the data are extremely limited or the features leak target information.

Capacity is therefore only one axis. Diagnosis should examine training curves, per-group performance, calibration, data quality, optimization statistics, and the process by which the model was selected.

### Explicit and implicit regularization

Regularization modifies either the objective, the architecture, the data, or the optimization procedure so that some solutions are preferred over others.

An $L_2$-regularized objective is

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\min_w
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i(w)
+\lambda\|w\|_2^2.
$$
</div>

The penalty discourages large Euclidean parameter norms and, in linear models, improves conditioning. It usually shrinks coefficients continuously rather than setting many exactly to zero.

An $L_1$-regularized objective is

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\min_w
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i(w)
+\lambda\|w\|_1.
$$
</div>

The nonsmooth geometry of the $L_1$ ball promotes coordinatewise sparsity. At zero, $|w_j|$ does not have an ordinary derivative. Its subdifferential is

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\partial |w_j|
=
\begin{cases}
\{1\}, & w_j\gt0,\\
[-1,1], & w_j=0,\\
\{-1\}, & w_j\lt0.
\end{cases}
$$
</div>

Assigning a derivative of zero at the origin is an implementation convention, not a proof of differentiability. Proximal gradient and coordinate-descent methods exploit the correct nonsmooth structure directly.

Other regularizers act differently.

- **Early stopping** limits how far optimization travels toward a training-set optimum.
- **Data augmentation** encodes invariances by generating transformed examples with preserved labels.
- **Dropout** randomly removes activations during training, discouraging brittle co-adaptation. It must be disabled during evaluation, with the appropriate scaling convention handled by the implementation.
- **Label smoothing** replaces hard one-hot targets with a slightly softened distribution and can reduce overconfidence.
- **Architectural constraints** such as convolution, parameter sharing, equivariance, low rank, or monotonicity restrict the functions that can be represented.

Optimization itself also imposes implicit preferences. In an underdetermined linear least-squares problem, gradient descent initialized at zero converges to a minimum-norm solution under standard conditions. The selected solution is shaped by the algorithm even without an explicit penalty.

## Optimizers and Learning-Rate Schedules

An optimizer is not primarily a device for "escaping local minima." It defines how gradient information, noise, curvature surrogates, and history are converted into parameter motion.

### Stochastic gradient descent and momentum

Basic stochastic gradient descent uses the current minibatch gradient $g_k$. Momentum supplements that gradient with a moving direction:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\text{SGD: }\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta_k g_k,
\qquad
\text{momentum: }v_{k+1}=\beta v_k+(1-\beta)g_k,\quad
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta_k v_{k+1}.
$$
</div>

Conventions differ by a constant scaling of $v$. The essential effect is temporal averaging. Directions that persist accumulate, while rapidly alternating directions partially cancel. Momentum can accelerate motion along shallow valleys and reduce oscillation across steep ones.

### Adaptive preconditioning

AdaGrad, RMSprop, Adam, and related methods scale coordinates according to gradient history. Adam tracks first and second moment estimates in parallel:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
m_k=\beta_1m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_k,
\qquad
v_k=\beta_2v_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2,
$$
</div>

with elementwise square and division after bias correction. The denominator acts as a diagonal preconditioner. Coordinates with persistently large squared gradients receive smaller effective steps.

Adam is often easy to tune, but it is not uniformly superior to SGD, and adaptive scaling does not remove the need for an appropriate learning rate. AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive gradient update. This distinction matters because adding an $L_2$ gradient term inside Adam is not generally equivalent to multiplicative weight decay after coordinatewise preconditioning.

### Scheduling the step size

A constant learning rate can be useful during an exploratory phase, while a decaying rate reduces late-stage noise. Two common smooth schedules are exponential and cosine decay:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\eta_k^{\mathrm{exp}}=\eta_0\gamma^k,
\qquad
\eta_k^{\mathrm{cos}}
=\eta_{\min}+\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})
\left(1+\cos\frac{\pi k}{K}\right).
$$
</div>

Piecewise schedules instead reduce the rate at selected epochs. The formulas differ, but the shared purpose is to allow larger exploratory motion early and more stable refinement later.

Warmup begins with smaller steps and increases toward the nominal rate. It is especially useful when early gradients, normalization statistics, or optimizer moments are poorly calibrated.

Batch size and learning rate interact, but simple linear or square-root scaling rules are heuristics tied to assumptions about gradient noise and training regime. A batch-size change should be validated rather than treated as an algebraic guarantee.

## Classification Metrics and Decision Costs

A probabilistic classifier produces scores or probabilities. A decision rule converts them into actions, often through a threshold. Loss, ranking quality, calibration, and thresholded classification quality are different properties.

For binary classification, define true positives $TP$, false positives $FP$, false negatives $FN$, and true negatives $TN$. Then

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\operatorname{accuracy}
=
\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FP+FN+TN},
$$
</div>

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\operatorname{precision}
=
\frac{TP}{TP+FP},
\qquad
\operatorname{recall}
=
\frac{TP}{TP+FN},
$$
</div>

<div class="display-equation">
$$
F_1
=
\frac{2\,\operatorname{precision}\,\operatorname{recall}}
{\operatorname{precision}+\operatorname{recall}}.
$$
</div>

Specificity is $TN/(TN+FP)$. Balanced accuracy averages recall and specificity and is less dominated by the majority class.

Accuracy can be nearly useless for rare-event detection. A classifier that always predicts the majority class may have excellent accuracy and zero recall for the event of interest. Precision also depends on prevalence. Even with fixed sensitivity and specificity, the fraction of positive predictions that are correct changes when the base rate changes.

Threshold-free summaries add other perspectives. ROC-AUC measures ranking across false-positive and true-positive rates. Precision-recall curves are often more revealing when the positive class is rare. Neither directly measures probability calibration. A calibrated model should satisfy the approximate frequency statement that among cases assigned probability $0.7$, about $70\%$ experience the event, within the relevant population and sampling uncertainty.

No metric is universally authoritative. The right criterion follows from decision costs. Missing a dangerous condition, triggering an unnecessary intervention, rejecting a qualified applicant, and displaying a poor recommendation carry different consequences. Model evaluation should state whose errors matter and how they are weighted.

## Multiclass Linear Classification

With $C$ mutually exclusive classes, a linear classifier produces one logit per class:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
z=W x+b,
\qquad
z_c=w_c^\top x+b_c,
\qquad
W\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d},\quad b\in\mathbb{R}^C.
$$
</div>

Each row $w_c^\top$ supplies one class score. All class parameters are optimized jointly, and the predicted class is usually $\arg\max_c z_c$.

### Softmax and categorical cross-entropy

Softmax maps logits to a categorical distribution. Because adding a common constant to all logits changes no probability, implementations subtract the maximum logit $m$ before exponentiation:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
p_c
=
\frac{e^{z_c-m}}
{\sum_j e^{z_j-m}},
\qquad
m=\max_j z_j.
$$
</div>

For a one-hot target $y$, categorical cross-entropy and its logit derivative are

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\ell(z,y)
=
-\sum_{c=1}^C y_c\log p_c,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_c}
=
p_c-y_c.
$$
</div>

If the true class is $t$, the loss reduces to $-\log p_t$. The compact gradient says exactly how learning redistributes score: it pushes the true class upward and every competing class downward in proportion to its current probability.

The classes compete through the shared denominator. Increasing one class probability necessarily changes the others. This is different from fitting $C$ independent sigmoid classifiers, which is appropriate for multi-label tasks where several labels may be true simultaneously.

A scalar target such as the digit value $7$ should not be treated as a regression coordinate merely because the label is written as a number. Class labels are names without arithmetic distance. One-hot or class-index representations preserve that semantics; regression would incorrectly claim, for example, that class eight lies twice as far from class six as class seven does.

**The learning loop in one view.**

A supervised classification system repeats four operations:

1. Compute logits and a differentiable loss in a forward pass.
2. Apply reverse-mode automatic differentiation to obtain parameter gradients.
3. Let the optimizer update parameters according to its state and current learning rate.
4. Evaluate held-out behavior without updating parameters.

Training ends according to a declared budget or model-selection rule. Prediction retains the forward computation but removes gradient recording and parameter updates. This closes the classification story: an affine map creates scores, a probabilistic link interprets them, a likelihood supplies the loss, and optimization adjusts the map while held-out data check whether the learned boundary generalizes.

## Linear Regression

Classification predicts discrete alternatives. Regression keeps the same affine machinery but changes the meaning of its output. The prediction is now a quantity whose numerical magnitude and distance matter: temperature, energy consumption, reaction time, or future demand. That semantic change also changes the loss. A mistake of two units may reasonably count more than a mistake of one, whereas the numeric names of two classes carry no such distance.

Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ contain one observation per row and let $y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ collect the targets. The affine prediction and ordinary least-squares objective can be written together as

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\widehat y=Xw+b\mathbf 1,
\qquad
J(w,b)=\frac{1}{2n}\|Xw+b\mathbf 1-y\|_2^2.
$$
</div>

The word *linear* refers to the learned coefficients; the intercept makes the input-output map affine. The factor $1/2$ is merely a derivative convenience. Centering every feature column and the target separates the intercept from the other coefficients, leaving the centered problem $J(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2/(2n)$.

### Least squares as geometry and optimization

Differentiation reveals why least squares is unusually transparent:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\nabla J(w)=\frac{1}{n}X^\top(Xw-y),
\qquad
\nabla^2J(w)=\frac{1}{n}X^\top X,
\qquad
X^\top X\widehat w=X^\top y.
$$
</div>

The Hessian is positive semidefinite, so every local minimum is global. The final equality is the normal equation. It has a geometric interpretation that is more important than memorizing the algebra: at the optimum, the residual $r=y-X\widehat w$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$. The fitted vector is therefore the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the feature space spanned by those columns.

When $X$ has full column rank, this projection has a unique coefficient vector, conventionally displayed as $\widehat w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. That expression explains the estimator but should not be read as a numerical recipe. Forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number, and explicitly constructing an inverse performs unnecessary work. QR factorization is normally more stable for a full-rank problem. SVD is more informative when rank deficiency or near-collinearity matters because it exposes the weakly determined directions directly.

If $X$ is rank deficient, several coefficient vectors may generate the same fitted values. The Moore-Penrose choice $\widehat w=X^+y$ selects the least-squares solution with minimum Euclidean norm. This is a canonical computational convention, not evidence that the individual coefficients have uniquely identified scientific meanings. Prediction can remain stable even while coefficient interpretation is fragile.

**Statistical interpretation.** Suppose the observations follow $Y=Xw+\varepsilon$ with independent Gaussian noise $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I)$. Under that model, minimizing squared error is the same as maximizing likelihood. The familiar loss therefore carries assumptions: errors are centered, have constant conditional variance, and have tails compatible with a Gaussian description.

Least squares can still be useful when those assumptions are imperfect, but the consequences differ. Heteroscedasticity may leave point predictions useful while invalidating naive standard errors. Correlated observations reduce the effective information in the sample. A single extreme residual receives a quadratic penalty and may dominate the fit. Absolute error, Huber loss, quantile loss, weighted least squares, or an explicit heavy-tailed likelihood can be better matched to the observation process. Loss selection is thus part of modeling, not a cosmetic choice made after the model is chosen.

### Ridge: stabilizing uncertain directions

Collinearity creates a characteristic problem: several coefficient combinations fit almost equally well, so a small change in data can produce a large change in $w$. Ridge regression responds by adding a quadratic preference for smaller coefficients:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
J_\lambda(w)=\frac{1}{2}\|Xw-y\|_2^2+rac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_2^2,
\qquad
\widehat w_\lambda=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y,\quad \lambda\gt0.
$$
</div>

The added term changes both statistics and geometry. For every nonzero $v$, the quadratic form $v^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I)v$ equals $\|Xv\|_2^2+\lambda\|v\|_2^2$ and is strictly positive. The regularized objective therefore has a unique minimizer even when the original design is rank deficient, and its linear system is better conditioned.

The SVD makes the shrinkage selective rather than mysterious. If $X=U\Sigma V^\top$, ridge multiplies the data coordinate associated with singular value $\sigma_j$ by $\sigma_j/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)$. Well-supported directions change relatively little; directions with tiny singular values shrink strongly. Ridge does not recover information destroyed by collinearity. It accepts bias in exchange for lower variance and more stable prediction.

The penalty strength belongs to the validation process. It should not be tuned against the final test set. The intercept is usually left unpenalized, and features are often standardized before applying one common $\lambda$, because the numerical size of a coefficient depends on the unit in which its feature is measured.

### Lasso: sparsity through nonsmoothness

Lasso replaces the quadratic penalty with an $L_1$ penalty. Its key mathematics can be summarized without pretending that the objective is differentiable at zero:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\min_w\frac{1}{2}\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_1,
\qquad
0\in X^\top(Xw-y)+\lambda\,\partial\|w\|_1.
$$
</div>

The interval-valued subgradient at zero is exactly what allows a coefficient to remain zero while still satisfying optimality. In the special case of an orthonormal design, each unregularized coefficient $a$ is transformed by the soft-threshold rule $\operatorname{sign}(a)\max(|a|-\lambda,0)$. Coordinate-descent and proximal-gradient algorithms generalize this thresholding logic to ordinary designs.

Sparsity can make a predictor easier to inspect and cheaper to deploy, but it is not automatic causal discovery. When several features carry nearly the same signal, a small sample perturbation may change which one lasso retains. The selected support may be unstable even when predictions are stable. Elastic-net regularization combines $L_1$ selection with $L_2$ shrinkage when grouped, correlated features should be treated less competitively.

### Nonlinearity through representation

Linear regression is not limited to straight lines in the original coordinates. It remains linear in its parameters after a fixed feature map $\phi$: one may fit $f_w(x)=w^\top\phi(x)$ using polynomial terms, splines, Fourier features, or kernel-induced representations. Polynomial regression, for example, uses $\phi(x)=(1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^p)^\top$ while retaining a least-squares objective in $w$.

This observation connects regression back to classification. A linear predictor is only as expressive as the representation it receives. Hand-designed features move the modeling question from "which nonlinear optimizer should be used?" to "which coordinates make a simple predictor appropriate?" They can be powerful when they encode genuine structure, but a high-degree polynomial may fit the observed interval and oscillate violently outside it. Extrapolation behavior, units, invariances, and domain knowledge matter as much as training error.

Fixed features also reveal the point at which a learned representation becomes attractive. If the useful transformation is unknown and must itself be inferred from data, the model needs trainable stages before the final linear predictor. Merely stacking more affine maps will not solve that problem; the next section explains why.

## Why Depth Requires Nonlinearity

Stacking affine maps without nonlinear activations does not create a more expressive function. Two consecutive layers collapse algebraically into one:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
h=W_1x+b_1,
\qquad
y=W_2h+b_2,
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
y=(W_2W_1)x+(W_2b_1+b_2),
$$
</div>

which is another affine map. Any finite composition of affine maps collapses in the same way.

A multilayer perceptron inserts nonlinear activations:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
h_1=\phi_1(W_1x+b_1),
\qquad
h_2=\phi_2(W_2h_1+b_2),
\qquad
z=W_3h_2+b_3.
$$
</div>

The hidden representation changes which points can be separated by the final linear layer. Depth allows repeated reuse and composition of intermediate features, although expressive power alone does not guarantee trainability or generalization.

### Activation functions

Sigmoid and hyperbolic tangent are smooth and bounded, while ReLU keeps the positive half-line unchanged:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}},
\qquad
\tanh(x)=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}},
\qquad
\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x).
$$
</div>

The derivatives of sigmoid and tanh approach zero for inputs of large magnitude. Across many layers, repeated multiplication by small derivatives can weaken the gradient reaching early parameters. ReLU avoids saturation on its positive side and is inexpensive, but a unit that remains negative for all relevant inputs receives zero local gradient and may become inactive. Leaky ReLU retains a small slope $\alpha$ on that side instead of setting it exactly to zero.

GELU and SiLU use smooth input-dependent gates. They are common in modern architectures, but no activation is uniformly best. Its range, derivative, smoothness, computational cost, and interaction with initialization and normalization all matter.

**Initialization and signal propagation.**

Deep networks repeatedly multiply activations and gradients by weight matrices. If typical singular values are far above one, magnitudes can grow rapidly; if they are far below one, they can decay.

Variance-scaled initialization chooses weight variance according to fan-in and sometimes fan-out. Xavier-style initialization is suited to approximately symmetric activations, while He-style initialization accounts for the fraction of ReLU activations that are zero. These rules do not make optimization trivial. They place the initial network in a regime where signals are less likely to vanish or explode immediately.

## Normalization

Input features measured in incompatible units can produce severely anisotropic objectives. A single learning rate must then negotiate directions with very different curvature. Input standardization often improves conditioning.

Min-max scaling maps a declared range to a target interval. Z-score standardization subtracts the training mean and divides by the training standard deviation:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\widehat{x}_j
=
\frac{x_j-\mu_j}{\sqrt{\sigma_j^2+\varepsilon}}.
$$
</div>

The statistics must be estimated on training data and then reused on validation, test, and deployment data. Recomputing them from a test set leaks information and makes predictions depend on the evaluation batch.

### Batch normalization

For feature $j$ in a minibatch $B$, batch normalization computes the batch mean and variance, then restores learnable scale and shift:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\mu_{B,j}
=
\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}x_{ij},
\qquad
\sigma_{B,j}^2
=
\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}(x_{ij}-\mu_{B,j})^2,
\qquad
y_{ij}
=
\gamma_j
\frac{x_{ij}-\mu_{B,j}}
{\sqrt{\sigma_{B,j}^2+\varepsilon}}
+\beta_j.
$$
</div>

The learnable scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$ preserve representational flexibility. During evaluation, standard BatchNorm implementations normally use running statistics accumulated during training. Consequently, `train()` and `eval()` modes are behaviorally different.

BatchNorm was originally motivated by reducing internal covariate shift, but later work showed that this story is incomplete. Its benefits are better understood through several interacting effects: reparameterization, smoother or better-conditioned optimization along training trajectories, scale invariance, tolerance of larger learning rates, and stochastic regularization from batch statistics.

There is no universal rule that BatchNorm must always appear before or after an activation. Both arrangements exist, and residual architectures distinguish pre-activation and post-activation designs. Placement is an architectural choice that must be stated, not a theorem derived from the word "input."

Small or nonrepresentative batches make batch statistics noisy. Distributed training also raises the question of whether statistics are local or synchronized across devices.

### Layer normalization

Layer normalization computes statistics across selected feature dimensions within each example rather than across the minibatch:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\mu_i=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=1}^D x_{ij},
\qquad
\sigma_i^2=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=1}^D(x_{ij}-\mu_i)^2.
$$
</div>

It then applies the same normalize-and-affine pattern. Because it does not depend on other examples in the batch and uses input statistics in both training and evaluation, LayerNorm is well suited to sequence models, online inference, and variable batch sizes. It is not simply BatchNorm with batch size one; the normalized axes and affine parameterization are different.

Normalization is a reparameterization and statistical operation, not a substitute for inspecting invalid values, poorly scaled targets, unstable losses, or inappropriate optimization.

## Residual Connections

A residual block learns a correction to the identity. Its forward map and local Jacobian are

<div class="display-equation">
$$
y=x+F(x;\theta),
\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}
=
I+J_F(x).
$$
</div>

This form requires matching input and output shapes. The identity contribution gives gradients a direct route through each block, so a chain of residual blocks multiplies factors of the form $I+J_{F_k}(x_k)$ rather than unrelated unconstrained Jacobians. Residual connections do not guarantee nonvanishing gradients, but they make identity-like propagation representationally and dynamically accessible.

When shapes differ, a projection may replace the identity shortcut. The shortcut then preserves a direct route while adapting dimension.

Residual design also changes the function class. Setting $F$ near zero makes a block close to identity, so adding depth need not immediately destroy a useful representation. This helps optimization, but deeper networks still require appropriate initialization, normalization, step sizes, and computational budgets.

## Computation Graphs

A computation graph is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are elementary operations and whose edges carry tensors. Forward evaluation follows a topological order. Reverse-mode differentiation traverses the graph backward, applying local derivative rules and summing contributions at branches.

For a small scalar example, consider the forward computation $a=3$, $b=4$, $c=a+b$, $d=2c$, and $L=d^2$. The values are $c=7$, $d=14$, and $L=196$. Reverse propagation can be read in one line:

<div class="display-equation">
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial d}=2d=28,
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial c}=28\cdot2=56,
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}
=56.
$$
</div>

The multiplication node scales the incoming derivative by its local factor, while the addition node sends the same upstream gradient to both parents. This tiny example is the same mechanism used by a network with millions of operations; only the graph and tensor dimensions are larger.

Intermediate tensors required for local backward rules must remain available, which creates a memory cost. Gradient checkpointing trades additional forward recomputation for reduced activation storage. In-place modification can invalidate saved values and must obey the framework's autograd rules.

The graph abstraction unifies chains, branches, residual paths, and shared parameters. If one parameter is used at several nodes, its gradient is the sum of all path contributions. This is why one general reverse-mode engine can train architectures whose forward structures look very different.

## From the Mathematics to a PyTorch Program

The preceding sections describe a complete learning system in mathematical language. A PyTorch program should preserve the same structure visibly. Data loading defines which observations are available. The model defines the forward map. The loss turns outputs into a scalar objective. Autograd computes derivatives. The optimizer updates parameters. Validation selects a model, and the test set evaluates that selection once.

The example below trains a small multilayer perceptron on MNIST. It is deliberately modest. Its purpose is to make every stage inspectable, not to claim state-of-the-art image recognition.

### Reproducibility and device selection

We begin by making the random split repeatable and selecting an available device.

```python
import copy
import random

import numpy as np
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.optim import AdamW
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import CosineAnnealingLR
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, random_split
from torchvision import datasets, transforms


SEED = 42
random.seed(SEED)
np.random.seed(SEED)
torch.manual_seed(SEED)

device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")
```

A fixed seed makes the split and pseudorandom sequence easier to reproduce in one environment. It does not promise bitwise identity across PyTorch versions, hardware platforms, or every parallel kernel. Reproducibility is a controlled experimental condition, not a magical property of one integer.

### Data preparation without test leakage

MNIST already provides an official training partition and an official test partition. We split only the original training partition into training and validation subsets. The official test data remain untouched until model selection is complete.

The transform first converts each image to a tensor and then standardizes it with training-distribution constants commonly used for MNIST. In a new dataset, those statistics should be estimated from the training partition alone.

```python
transform = transforms.Compose(
    [
        transforms.ToTensor(),
        transforms.Normalize((0.1307,), (0.3081,)),
    ]
)

full_train = datasets.MNIST(
    root="./data",
    train=True,
    download=True,
    transform=transform,
)
test_dataset = datasets.MNIST(
    root="./data",
    train=False,
    download=True,
    transform=transform,
)

split_generator = torch.Generator().manual_seed(SEED)
train_dataset, val_dataset = random_split(
    full_train,
    [55_000, 5_000],
    generator=split_generator,
)

BATCH_SIZE = 128
pin_memory = device.type == "cuda"

train_loader = DataLoader(
    train_dataset,
    batch_size=BATCH_SIZE,
    shuffle=True,
    num_workers=0,
    pin_memory=pin_memory,
)
val_loader = DataLoader(
    val_dataset,
    batch_size=BATCH_SIZE,
    shuffle=False,
    num_workers=0,
    pin_memory=pin_memory,
)
test_loader = DataLoader(
    test_dataset,
    batch_size=BATCH_SIZE,
    shuffle=False,
    num_workers=0,
    pin_memory=pin_memory,
)
```

Training data are shuffled because each epoch should form fresh minibatches. Validation and test data need no shuffle because their order does not change aggregate metrics. Setting `num_workers=0` favors portability, especially on Windows and in notebooks; larger projects should benchmark parallel loading rather than copy this value blindly.

The shape entering the model is `(batch, 1, 28, 28)`. The first layer expects 784 features, so `nn.Flatten()` converts each image to `(batch, 784)` while preserving the batch dimension.

### Defining the model

```python
class MNISTMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        super().__init__()
        self.network = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Flatten(),
            nn.Linear(28 * 28, 256),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Dropout(p=0.10),
            nn.Linear(256, 128),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, 10),
        )

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        return self.network(x)


model = MNISTMLP().to(device)
```

The two hidden ReLUs prevent the three linear layers from collapsing into one affine map. Dropout supplies a mild training-time regularizer. The final layer returns ten unrestricted logits.

There is intentionally no Softmax module at the end. `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` expects unnormalized logits and internally performs the stable log-softmax and negative-log-likelihood calculation. Applying Softmax first would duplicate work, lose numerical stability, and give the loss a different input than its documented contract.

### Loss, optimizer, and schedule

```python
NUM_EPOCHS = 12

criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
optimizer = AdamW(
    model.parameters(),
    lr=1e-3,
    weight_decay=1e-4,
)
scheduler = CosineAnnealingLR(
    optimizer,
    T_max=NUM_EPOCHS,
    eta_min=1e-5,
)
```

Cross-entropy matches the categorical output model developed earlier. AdamW supplies adaptive moment estimates with decoupled weight decay. The cosine schedule lowers the learning rate over the declared training budget.

These choices are a coherent baseline, not universally optimal constants. The learning rate, weight decay, hidden widths, dropout probability, batch size, and number of epochs are hyperparameters. They may be selected with validation data; they must not be tuned against the final test result.

### One training epoch

The training loop should make the state transition explicit. `model.train()` enables training behavior such as dropout. Each minibatch is transferred to the device, gradients are cleared, logits and loss are computed, backward propagation fills parameter gradients, and the optimizer updates parameters.

```python
def train_one_epoch(
    model: nn.Module,
    loader: DataLoader,
    criterion: nn.Module,
    optimizer: torch.optim.Optimizer,
    device: torch.device,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    model.train()
    loss_sum = 0.0
    correct = 0
    count = 0

    for images, labels in loader:
        images = images.to(device, non_blocking=True)
        labels = labels.to(device, non_blocking=True)

        optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        logits = model(images)
        loss = criterion(logits, labels)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

        batch_size = labels.size(0)
        loss_sum += loss.item() * batch_size
        correct += (logits.argmax(dim=1) == labels).sum().item()
        count += batch_size

    return loss_sum / count, correct / count
```

Gradients accumulate by default in PyTorch, so they must be cleared before the next backward pass unless intentional accumulation is being used. `set_to_none=True` can avoid unnecessary writes and makes an untouched gradient distinguishable from a computed zero.

The loss is multiplied by batch size before accumulation because `CrossEntropyLoss` returns a batch mean by default. Averaging those means without weighting would bias the epoch loss when the final batch is smaller.

### Validation is not training without updates

Evaluation needs two independent switches. `model.eval()` changes modules such as Dropout and BatchNorm to evaluation behavior. `torch.inference_mode()` disables gradient recording and additional autograd bookkeeping. Neither operation replaces the other.

```python
@torch.inference_mode()
def evaluate(
    model: nn.Module,
    loader: DataLoader,
    criterion: nn.Module,
    device: torch.device,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    model.eval()
    loss_sum = 0.0
    correct = 0
    count = 0

    for images, labels in loader:
        images = images.to(device, non_blocking=True)
        labels = labels.to(device, non_blocking=True)

        logits = model(images)
        loss = criterion(logits, labels)

        batch_size = labels.size(0)
        loss_sum += loss.item() * batch_size
        correct += (logits.argmax(dim=1) == labels).sum().item()
        count += batch_size

    return loss_sum / count, correct / count
```

Using `outputs.data` is unnecessary and can encourage unsafe habits around autograd. Prediction should use the tensor directly, or an explicitly detached tensor when detachment is required.

### Selecting the best validation checkpoint

The final loop trains, validates, updates the schedule, and keeps the state with the lowest validation loss.

```python
best_val_loss = float("inf")
best_state = None

for epoch in range(1, NUM_EPOCHS + 1):
    train_loss, train_acc = train_one_epoch(
        model, train_loader, criterion, optimizer, device
    )
    val_loss, val_acc = evaluate(
        model, val_loader, criterion, device
    )

    if val_loss < best_val_loss:
        best_val_loss = val_loss
        best_state = copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict())

    scheduler.step()

    print(
        f"epoch={epoch:02d} "
        f"train_loss={train_loss:.4f} "
        f"train_acc={train_acc:.3%} "
        f"val_loss={val_loss:.4f} "
        f"val_acc={val_acc:.3%}"
    )

if best_state is None:
    raise RuntimeError("Training completed without a checkpoint.")

model.load_state_dict(best_state)
test_loss, test_acc = evaluate(
    model, test_loader, criterion, device
)
print(f"test_loss={test_loss:.4f} test_acc={test_acc:.3%}")
```

The test result appears only after the validation-based checkpoint has been chosen. If the test score is then used to alter the architecture or hyperparameters, it is no longer a final test. The entire selection process should be rerun against a new untouched evaluation source.

### A compact debugging checklist

When a training run fails, checking invariants is usually more useful than immediately changing the optimizer.

- Confirm that inputs, labels, logits, and loss have the intended shapes and dtypes.
- Inspect a batch after every preprocessing stage and verify that labels still match examples.
- Check whether loss on a tiny subset can be driven near zero. Failure often indicates an implementation or optimization problem.
- Monitor gradient norms, parameter norms, learning rate, nonfinite values, and class frequencies.
- Compare training and validation curves rather than a single final number.
- Verify that evaluation calls both `eval()` and a no-gradient or inference context.
- Confirm that validation and test examples, groups, and preprocessing statistics did not influence training.
- Compare against a simple linear baseline before attributing gains to a deeper architecture.

The purpose of this checklist is not ceremonial reproducibility. It connects each software symptom to the mathematical pipeline developed throughout the chapter.

## The Role of the Linear Layer

We began with one affine score and arrived at a complete learning system. The path was not accidental.

A linear layer first turned data into geometry: a weight vector defined a direction, a bias located a boundary, and a margin measured separation. A probabilistic output then connected that score to a data model. The loss converted likelihood into an objective, and the chain rule converted the objective into parameter gradients. Smoothness explained when a gradient step must decrease the objective, while statistical reasoning explained why decreasing training loss is not enough.

The same layer then appeared in regression, where least squares, ridge, and lasso exposed identifiability, conditioning, shrinkage, and sparsity. Stacking linear layers revealed why nonlinearity is indispensable. Normalization, initialization, and residual paths showed that representational power must be accompanied by controlled signal and gradient propagation. Finally, the computation graph turned those mathematical dependencies into an executable reverse-mode algorithm.

The linear layer is therefore not merely the easiest neural-network component. It is the point at which representation, probability, optimization, statistics, and software first become one coherent mechanism.
